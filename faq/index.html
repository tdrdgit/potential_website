Questions asked
before the first order.

Written for people who already know what FF&E is: developers, retail network directors and contractors who have run a package before and know where it usually goes wrong. Nothing here is a definition for its own sake — each answer is what we would say in a first meeting, in the same words.

An FF&E procurement partner sits between all three as a single point of contact, and represents the owner's interest rather than a supplier's. The designer owns the design intent, the general contractor owns the building works, and the FF&E package sits across both without belonging to either — which is where it usually goes wrong.

Potential does not manufacture and does not design. It manages the design intent, the budget, the tender and the delivery across a pre-selected network of Italian suppliers, in the Owner's Representative role.

A pre-construction FF&E audit checks whether the package can actually be bought at the budget it carries: whether the drawings and specifications are complete enough to price, where the specified scope and the allowance diverge, and which open technical questions will block procurement if they stay open.

The drawing and spec review reads the FF&E schedule against the architectural and MEP drawings and flags what is missing or contradictory — a finish code with no reference, a loose item with no power or drainage behind it, a performance requirement such as fire rating or rub count stated in one document and absent from another.

The budget gap analysis prices the specification as written against the allowance carried in the development budget, so the gap is a number before the tender rather than a surprise at bid opening. Every unresolved question goes into an RFI risk log with a decision owner and the date after which the answer starts to cost money.

At this stage changes are drawing revisions. Later, the same changes are change orders.

A structured FF&E tender is one where every invited supplier prices the same scope, on the same commercial terms, in the same return format — so the comparison at the end is genuinely like-for-like rather than a list of numbers that mean different things.

Prequalification comes first. The panel is a pre-selected group of Italian manufacturers with certified quality and stated lead times, and per package the question is narrower than whether a factory is good: has it produced this item type, in this finish, at this volume, on this kind of programme.

The RFQ package then fixes what is being priced — BOQ with item codes matching the drawings, specifications, samples required, delivery terms, milestone dates, and the format in which the price must come back. Bid leveling normalises what suppliers price differently — prototypes and tooling, packing, transport, spare parts, installation supervision, payment terms — onto one sheet before anything is ranked.

The published outcome is 8-15% average tender savings on FF&E contract value, and it comes from comparability rather than from pressure on a single supplier.

Value engineering on an FF&E package changes how an item is made or sourced, not what the designer specified it to look like, feel like or perform like. The moment a proposal alters the visible or tactile result it is no longer value engineering: it is a design change, and it goes back to the designer as one.

The exercise is run on the lines that carry the cost, not on every line — a few item families usually absorb most of an FF&E budget, and those are the ones where the method actually moves the number.

Three levers, in order of how little they disturb the design: build the same item differently, through substrate, joinery method or edge detail; substitute a material inside the same performance class; consolidate similar items across areas so the volume is priced once. Each proposal reaches the designer with a physical sample or a technical comparison attached, before it is allowed into the budget.

The published reference figure for this work is €570K to €1.1M of documented savings on a single €6M project.

A change is priced and logged before it is executed, never after. The change order log records what changed, who requested it, the cost and programme impact, the contract line it hits, and the date by which the decision must be taken — and changes are separated by origin, because owner-driven, design-driven and site-driven changes have different commercial consequences.

Committed, invoiced and still-open stay three separate numbers rather than one balance that only becomes clear at the end. An overrun is rarely one large event: it is a series of small approvals that nobody totalled.

Expediting means checking production against the agreed milestone dates at the factory, item by item, instead of waiting for a supplier to report a delay — once a delay is reported it is usually no longer recoverable within the programme.

The inspection points that matter are few: approval of the first article before series production starts, a check during production while a defect is still cheap to correct, and a pre-shipment inspection against the approved sample, the specification and the packing list.

A defect found at the factory costs a re-make. The same defect found on site costs a re-make, plus freight, plus an installation window that has already been booked.

It changes one thing only — how quickly and how reliably information reaches the person who has to decide — and never the decision itself.

On an FF&E package the expensive failures are rarely failures of judgement, they are failures of timing: a budget is blown because the reconciliation between the BOQ, the issued orders and the current drawing revision happens three weeks after the money was committed. The clerical half of that reconciliation — matching lines, spotting what moved, flagging what no longer agrees — is the part worth automating, so a review starts from a short list of exceptions instead of a blank comparison.

What stays human is everything that carries a consequence: negotiating with a manufacturer, judging whether a sample is acceptable, deciding whether a delay is worth a substitution, choosing who gets the order. No system selects a supplier, sets a price or approves anything.

A spreadsheet is accurate on the day it is updated. The point of automating the clerical half is to remove the day.

It changes when you find out, not what you find out. Budget control on FF&E rarely fails because nobody is tracking; it fails because tracking is manual — the BOQ sits in one file, the purchase orders in another, the variations in a third, and the three agree only on the afternoon someone makes them agree.

Held against one line structure, the reconciliation stops being a task somebody has to remember. What reaches the owner is then not the whole model but the movements: which lines moved since the last report, what caused each one, and what the forecast at completion now says. Control means the number is early enough to still act on.

By watching the milestones inside the lead time, not the delivery date itself — that date is the last thing to move and the first thing everyone watches.

A stated lead time is made of steps: drawing approval, sample approval, material order, production slot, finishing, packing, collection. Each has a date, and each is a place where two weeks disappear without anyone announcing anything. When a step passes its date without confirmation, the order deserves a call — before the supplier has any reason to volunteer a delay.

The call is the work: establishing whether the cause is a material shortage, a saturated production slot, or an approval sitting on somebody's desk, frequently on the owner's side.

The same person who would be accountable without it. The Owner's Representative role and the single point of contact do not move because a tool produced the first draft of a number: nothing reaches an owner, a designer or a supplier without a person having reviewed it and being prepared to defend it.

A tool that proposes wrongly costs an hour. A tool trusted to decide costs a project, which is precisely why it is not allowed to.

A store format stays consistent when it is frozen as a procurement document rather than a design document: a specification precise enough that a different manufacturer, years later, produces the same result without reinterpreting it.

That means naming materials with supplier reference codes instead of generic descriptions, drawing junctions and tolerances instead of implying them, and keeping a physical benchmark sample of every finish against which deliveries are checked. Each new store is then bought against a control BOQ and compared with the format standard, not with the last store built.

Drift is rarely a design failure — it is a procurement failure: one substitution accepted on a single site to protect an opening date, then repeated because nobody recorded it. Every deviation is logged against the format specification and either rejected or absorbed into it, so the standard stays a living document instead of a folder of drawings from opening week.

For a repeating format, a framework agreement with call-off orders per store is normally better than tendering store by store: it converts the buy into one negotiation at programme volume and leaves only quantities, dimensions and dates to be fixed per site.

Tendering each store looks prudent and usually costs more — every tender restarts the learning curve, re-opens the specification to reinterpretation, and buys single-store quantities from suppliers who have to engineer the same item again.

The framework should fix unit prices per coded item, lead times by call-off quantity, the quality standard and the inspection regime, plus a stated mechanism for revising prices when a raw material moves, so a cost increase becomes a calculation rather than a renegotiation. The tender itself is run like any other buyout — prequalification, RFQ, bid leveling on a like-for-like basis — but priced on the programme rather than on the first store.

The risk to manage is the opposite one: a framework left untouched stops being a market price, so a re-benchmark date belongs in it from the start.

The first store carries the engineering, sampling and first-article time; every store after it is limited by production slot and transport, not by development. That is why a rollout calendar cannot be copied from a one-off project schedule: it has to be built backwards from the opening dates and forwards from available capacity at the same time.

A staggered calendar is almost always cheaper than a single opening date, because it lets each specialist produce in sequence instead of forcing joinery, metal, upholstery and lighting to peak in the same weeks. Where one date is fixed, the programme has to be produced earlier and stored — and storage belongs in the budget from the beginning, not in a change order.

Keeping an existing network in good condition is a supply continuity problem before it is a maintenance problem: what matters is whether the exact component specified at rollout can still be bought years later. Decors and laminates are discontinued, light sources and drivers change generation, fabrics leave the collection — and one obsolete part is enough to make a mismatch visible in a store that is otherwise intact.

The remedies are agreed at rollout rather than at the first breakage: a defined replacement stock, tooling retained at the manufacturer, and a pre-approved alternative for the components most likely to be withdrawn. When a component does disappear, the replacement is re-tendered against the original specification and compared with the retained benchmark instead of being approved from a photograph.

A generalist contract supplier gives you one contract and one margin over the same makers. A network of specialists gives you the manufacturer that performs one process every day — joinery, metal, upholstery, lighting, stone — and shifts the coordination burden onto whoever manages the network.

Italian manufacturing is organised that way by geography: specialist districts where a company's whole history is a single process, which is why a metal fabricator and a joinery workshop from two different districts will usually outperform one supplier attempting both.

The condition that makes it work is that the client still deals with one interface, and that finish quality is held constant at the manufacturer rather than at the store: first article approved against the retained benchmark, checks during the production run, inspection before shipping. Rejecting an item at the shopfront costs an opening date.
